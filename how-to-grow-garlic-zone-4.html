How to Grow Garlic in USDA Zone 4: A Complete Midwest Planting Guide

Garlic is one of the most rewarding crops for a cold-climate gardener. Plant individual cloves in fall, protect them through winter, and harvest full bulbs the following summer.

Garlic planting at a glance
  • When: Fall, generally one to two weeks after the first killing frost
  • Best types: Cold-hardy hardneck garlic
  • Spacing: About 6 inches between cloves
  • Depth: Set the base of each clove roughly 2–3 inches below the soil surface, point up
  • Mulch: 3–4 inches of clean straw or leaves
  • Harvest: Often late June through July, depending on local conditions

1. Choose garlic suited to a cold winter

For Midwest Zone 4 gardens, start with hardneck garlic, such as Porcelain, Purple Stripe, or Rocambole types. Hardneck garlic generally tolerates cold winters well and produces edible curled flower stalks called scapes. Buy healthy planting stock from a reputable garlic grower, preferably one experienced with northern climates. Grocery-store garlic may be poorly adapted or improperly stored for planting.

2. Know when to plant in Zone 4

Fall is the normal planting season. A useful rule is to plant about one to two weeks after the first killing frost, while the ground can still be worked. Exact timing varies across Zone 4 because hardiness zones describe winter lows, not local frost dates. In many Minnesota gardens, that means late September through October, depending on latitude and weather. The goal is root development before winter, without excessive aboveground growth.

3. Prepare a well-drained garden bed

Choose a spot receiving at least six hours of direct sunlight and where water does not stand. Garlic grows best in loose, moisture-retentive, well-drained soil with a pH around 6.0–7.0. Mix in finished compost if your soil needs organic matter. Avoid fresh manure. A soil test is the best guide for fertilizer decisions; don't automatically add bone meal or other phosphorus fertilizers unless a deficiency is indicated.

4. Separate and plant the cloves

  1. Break apart each garlic bulb into individual cloves a day or two before planting, keeping the papery skins intact.
  2. Choose the largest, firmest, undamaged cloves for planting. Save smaller cloves for cooking.
  3. Plant each clove with the pointed tip up and flat root plate down.
  4. Set the clove base about 2–3 inches below the soil surface, spacing cloves about 6 inches apart.
  5. Cover with soil and water the bed if the soil is dry.

Do not plant a whole garlic bulb as one unit if your goal is full-sized individual bulbs. Each planted clove normally produces one new bulb.

5. Mulch for winter protection

Cover the planted bed with roughly 3–4 inches of clean straw or shredded leaves. Mulch reduces freeze–thaw swings and helps suppress weeds. In spring, pull back heavy mulch if shoots are struggling to emerge; you can leave or replace a lighter layer later to retain moisture.

6. Care for garlic in spring and early summer

Keep weeds under control because garlic competes poorly with them. Supply consistent moisture during active growth, generally around an inch of water per week from rain and irrigation combined, adjusting for your soil and weather. Garlic needs nitrogen early in its growth; use soil-test recommendations and apply any needed nitrogen early in spring rather than late in the season. University of Minnesota Extension advises avoiding nitrogen after the first week of May because late feeding may delay bulbing.

Hardneck bonus: When garlic scapes curl, cut them off to direct energy toward bulb growth. Chop tender scapes into eggs, pesto, stir-fries, or pasta.

7. Harvest at the right time

Garlic often matures between late June and late July. Watch the leaves rather than relying only on the calendar: begin checking when the lower leaves brown but roughly half of the upper leaves remain green. Gently dig a test bulb. If cloves fill their wrappers, harvest the rest. Waiting too long can cause wrappers to split, reducing storage life.

8. Cure and store your garlic

Lift bulbs carefully with a garden fork, avoiding cuts and bruises. Brush off loose soil; don't wash the bulbs before curing. Keep bulbs with their tops attached in a shaded, dry, well-ventilated location for about 3–4 weeks. Once fully dry, trim roots and stems, then store whole bulbs in a cool, dry, airy location. Save your largest healthy bulbs for next fall's planting.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Planting too shallow: cloves are more vulnerable to winter damage.
  • Using soggy soil: poor drainage encourages rot.
  • Skipping weed control: weeds compete for moisture and nutrients.
  • Adding too much late nitrogen: this can delay bulbing.
  • Harvesting too late: split wrappers shorten storage life.

Frequently asked questions

Can I plant garlic in spring in Zone 4?

Fall planting is preferred because garlic benefits from a cold period and a longer growing season. Spring planting may produce smaller or poorly developed bulbs unless the planting stock has received appropriate cold treatment.

Should I soak garlic cloves before planting?

Soaking is not required for a successful crop. Start with clean, healthy planting stock and well-drained soil. Avoid unproven chemical or fertilizer soaks that could damage cloves.

Can I use garlic from the grocery store?

It may grow, but locally adapted seed garlic is a more dependable choice for northern winters.
