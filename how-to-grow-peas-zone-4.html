How to Grow Peas in USDA Zone 4: A Complete Midwest Guide

Make the most of cool Midwest spring weather with sweet shelling peas, crisp snow peas and sugar snap peas.

Peas at a glance

  • Sun: At least 6 hours daily
  • Season: Cool-season crop; direct sow early spring
  • Soil: Well-drained, pH about 6.0–7.0
  • Planting depth: About 1 inch
  • Spacing: Often 1–2 inches between seeds; follow variety instructions
  • Water: About 1 inch weekly, adjusted for conditions
  • Harvest: Often 55–75 days from sowing, variety dependent

1. Choose the right peas

Shelling peas are grown for the sweet peas inside their pods. Snow peas have flat, edible pods picked before the seeds swell. Sugar snap peas have thick, crunchy edible pods. For shorter Zone 4 springs, look for early-maturing varieties and disease resistance. Bush types need less support; tall vines appreciate a trellis.

2. When to plant in USDA Zones 3–6

Peas are cool-season vegetables. Direct sow in spring as soon as soil is workable and no longer waterlogged, often about 4–6 weeks before the average last frost. Seeds germinate in cool soil (roughly 40°F or above) but emerge faster as soil warms. Plants tolerate light frosts, though flowers and pods can be damaged by hard freezes. Planting dates vary greatly within a hardiness zone.

3. Prepare the soil and fertilizer

Choose a site with at least 6 hours of sunlight and loose, well-drained soil. Peas generally grow best around pH 6.0–7.0. Mix in finished compost if soil needs organic matter, but avoid excessive nitrogen fertilizer: peas are legumes and can obtain nitrogen through root-nodule bacteria when the correct rhizobia are present. A soil test helps determine whether phosphorus or potassium is needed. Inoculant labeled for peas may be helpful in soil where peas and related legumes have not been grown.

See our Soil & Fertilizer Guide before adding amendments.

4. How to plant peas

  1. Prepare a crumbly seedbed without working saturated soil.
  2. Direct sow seeds about 1 inch deep, generally 1–2 inches apart along a row; follow the seed packet for your variety and row spacing.
  3. Install a trellis or netting at planting for climbing types, before roots spread.
  4. Water after sowing and keep soil evenly moist until emergence; do not leave seeds in standing water.
  5. Mulch lightly after seedlings are established to conserve moisture and limit weeds.

Peas dislike root disturbance, so direct sowing is usually simpler than transplanting.

5. Watering and everyday care

Provide roughly 1 inch of water per week from rain and irrigation, adjusting for soil, wind, and temperature. Even moisture is especially important during flowering and pod fill. Water near the soil surface rather than soaking foliage, and avoid cultivating deeply around shallow roots. A trellis improves air circulation and makes harvesting easier.

6. Common problems in Midwest gardens

  • Poor germination: Cold, saturated soil can rot seeds; wait until soil is workable and drains well.
  • Yellowing plants: Check for waterlogging, root disease and nutrient problems before adding fertilizer.
  • Powdery mildew: White fungal growth is more likely as conditions favor disease; use resistant varieties and provide airflow.
  • Aphids: Check tender tips and undersides of leaves; encourage beneficial insects and use locally recommended controls when needed.
  • Few pods: Heat, drought and poor flowering conditions can reduce pod set.

Rotate pea plantings and remove diseased debris to help reduce soilborne disease pressure.

7. When and how to harvest

Many peas are ready in roughly 55–75 days, depending on the variety and weather. Pick shelling peas when pods are plump but still bright green; harvest snow peas while pods are flat and tender; pick snap peas when pods are full and crisp. Harvest every day or two during peak production, holding the vine with one hand to avoid pulling it loose. Refrigerate promptly for best sweetness.

8. Can you grow a fall crop?

Sometimes. Count backward from your expected first fall frost using the seed packet’s days to maturity, then allow extra time because autumn days are shorter. Sow in late summer when temperatures begin to moderate, keep seedbeds consistently moist, and choose early varieties. In Zone 4, fall peas are an experiment rather than a guaranteed harvest: late-summer heat can prevent establishment and early freezes may end production.

9. Mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting until summer heat to start a spring crop
  • Sowing into waterlogged soil
  • Adding high-nitrogen fertilizer without evidence it is needed
  • Skipping support for tall varieties
  • Letting pods become overmature before picking
  • Assuming USDA zone alone predicts planting dates

Frequently asked questions

Can peas survive frost?

Established peas tolerate some light frost, but severe freezes can injure plants and developing flowers or pods.

Do peas need fertilizer?

Often little nitrogen is needed in healthy soil. Use a soil test to guide other nutrients instead of applying a balanced fertilizer automatically.

Should I soak pea seeds?

Usually not necessary. Sowing into adequately moist soil is reliable; prolonged soaking may damage seeds.

Do peas need a trellis?

Short bush varieties may grow without one, but even compact types can benefit from support. Tall vines need it.
