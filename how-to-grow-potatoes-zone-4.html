How to Grow Potatoes in USDA Zone 4: A Complete Midwest Guide

From planting certified seed potatoes in spring to digging and storing your fall harvest, here's how to grow a productive potato crop in a short-season Midwest garden.

Potato growing at a glance

  • Sun: Full sun, ideally 6–8+ hours daily
  • Plant: In spring when soil is workable and warming, often April–May depending on location
  • Seed: Certified disease-free seed potatoes
  • Depth: About 3–5 inches
  • Spacing: 10–12 inches apart, rows about 30–36 inches apart
  • Water: Roughly 1 inch weekly, adjusted for rainfall and soil
  • Harvest: New potatoes in about 7–8 weeks; storage potatoes after vines die back

1. Choose the right potatoes

Choose early- or midseason varieties if your frost-free season is short. Red potatoes are excellent for boiling, russets for baking, and Yukon Gold–type potatoes are versatile in the kitchen. Look for varieties with resistance to diseases that have affected your garden.

Start with certified disease-free seed potatoes rather than grocery-store potatoes, which may carry disease or be treated to delay sprouting.

2. When to plant in Zones 3–6

Potatoes are a cool-season crop and can go into the ground before warm-season vegetables such as tomatoes and peppers. Wait until soil can be worked without becoming compacted and is beginning to warm. Use local soil conditions rather than hardiness zone alone to choose the date.

These are broad planning ranges, not guaranteed planting dates. A hardiness zone describes winter minimum temperatures, not a precise frost or soil-warming date.

3. Prepare the soil

Pick a sunny site with loose, fertile, well-drained soil. Potatoes grow especially well in sandy loam and generally prefer slightly acidic soil, around pH 6.0–6.5. A soil test will tell you whether nutrients or pH adjustments are needed. Finished compost can improve soil structure, but avoid fresh manure and waterlogged planting areas.

For more on soil testing and fertilizer nutrients, visit our Soil & Fertilizer Guide.

4. Prepare and plant seed potatoes

  1. Plant small seed potatoes whole. Cut larger ones into pieces roughly 1¼ inches across, each with at least one eye (two or three is preferable).
  2. Use a clean knife and allow cut surfaces to dry and toughen for a few days before planting.
  3. Plant pieces about 3–5 inches deep, 10–12 inches apart, with rows about 30–36 inches apart.
  4. Cover with soil, water as needed, and keep the bed evenly moist but not soggy.

5. Hill potatoes as they grow

Once stems reach roughly 12 inches tall, pull loose soil up around the base of each plant without burying all the leaves. Repeat once or twice as plants grow, building a total mound of about 6–8 inches. Hilling protects developing tubers from sunlight, which can turn them green.

6. Water and fertilize wisely

Supply consistent moisture, especially when tubers are forming. About an inch of water per week is a useful starting point, but sandy soil and hot weather may require more frequent watering. Avoid alternating drought and heavy soaking, which can cause misshapen or hollow potatoes.

Potatoes have substantial nutrient needs. Use a soil test to guide fertilizer choice and amount. Where additional nitrogen is needed, a side-dressing during early growth and hilling may help; avoid guessing at high fertilizer rates or adding phosphorus when the soil already has enough.

7. Prevent common pests and diseases

  • Colorado potato beetles: Check leaves, especially undersides, for eggs and larvae; remove small infestations promptly.
  • Potato scab: Rough corky patches on tubers; avoid unnecessary liming and keep soil moisture steady.
  • Early and late blight: Watch for unusual leaf spots and rapid plant decline; seek a local Extension diagnosis if symptoms appear.
  • Crop rotation: Avoid planting potatoes after tomatoes, peppers, eggplants or potatoes in the same bed for approximately 3–4 years when possible.

8. Harvest potatoes at the right time

For tender new potatoes, begin checking about seven to eight weeks after planting. For mature storage potatoes, wait until vines have dried down and skins are firm enough that they do not rub off easily. Loosen soil with a garden fork and lift tubers carefully to prevent bruises and cuts.

9. Cure and store your harvest

Keep mature, undamaged potatoes in a dark, well-ventilated place while skins finish setting, often around 10–14 days. Brush off dry soil rather than washing potatoes before storage. Store in a cool, dark, humid but ventilated environment, and check periodically for spoilage. Keep potatoes away from light to prevent greening. Do not eat sprouts or extensively green potatoes.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Planting in cold, waterlogged soil
  • Using uncertified or diseased seed potatoes
  • Letting developing tubers become exposed to sunlight
  • Overfertilizing without a soil test
  • Allowing large swings in soil moisture
  • Storing damaged or wet potatoes with healthy ones

Frequently asked questions

Can I grow potatoes in raised beds?

Yes. Use deep, well-drained soil, allow enough room for hilling, and check moisture more often because raised beds may dry out quickly.

Do potatoes need flowers to produce tubers?

No. Potato tubers form underground whether or not the plant blooms.

Why are some potatoes green?

Light exposure causes greening and can indicate elevated glycoalkaloids. Keep tubers covered while growing and store them in darkness; discard extensively green potatoes.
