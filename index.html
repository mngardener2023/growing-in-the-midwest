🍂 November: Protect Your Garden for Winter

Finish your fall cleanup, protect vulnerable plants, care for your soil, and prepare for another Midwest growing season.

November is a month of transition in the Midwest. The harvest is mostly complete, the days are shorter, and freezing temperatures are becoming a regular part of life. But there is still meaningful work to do! A little preparation now can help prevent winter damage and make spring gardening easier.

🧄 1. Check Your Fall-Planted Garlic

Garlic needs winter cold, but mulch helps moderate soil temperature changes and reduces frost heaving.

  • Check for roughly 3–6 inches of loose, clean straw or shredded-leaf mulch, adjusted for local conditions.
  • Look for exposed cloves and gently cover them with soil if needed.
  • Keep mulch from becoming densely packed or waterlogged.
  • Mark the beds so you can find them in spring.
Midwest tip: Zones 3–4 often benefit from consistent winter insulation. In Zones 5–6, avoid excessive wet mulch. Fall garlic shoots are not automatically a problem.

🍓 2. Protect Strawberries & Perennials

Winter mulch protects roots and crowns against severe cold and repeated freezing and thawing. Timing matters: don't cover strawberries too early.

For June-bearing strawberries

  1. Wait until plants are dormant after several hard frosts and the soil is consistently near freezing.
  2. Apply approximately 4–6 inches of loose, clean straw.
  3. Avoid heavy, matted leaves that hold too much moisture.
  4. In spring, gradually move mulch away from crowns as growth resumes; keep it nearby for cold snaps.

Also check young berry plants, rhubarb, asparagus, chives, thyme and oregano. Their winter needs vary by plant, age, drainage and variety.

Midwest tip: Keep mulch away from woody stems and sensitive crowns to reduce trapped moisture and rot.

🍂 3. Clean Up Vegetable Garden Beds

Remove severely diseased plant debris, rotting fruits and weeds with mature seeds. Disease-bearing material may not be suitable for a home compost pile that does not reach sufficiently high temperatures.

Not everything needs to go: healthy leaves and some standing stems can shelter beneficial insects and protect soil. Leave suitable habitat where it won't worsen known pest or disease problems.

Midwest tip: Avoid tilling saturated or frozen soil. Protecting soil structure matters more than making every bed look spotless.

🌱 4. Keep Composting

Compost activity slows in the cold and may stop temporarily when the pile freezes. Decomposition resumes when temperatures rise.

  • Add healthy leaves, vegetable scraps, coffee grounds and untreated shredded cardboard.
  • Balance wet, nitrogen-rich materials with dry, carbon-rich leaves.
  • Save bags of dry leaves for winter additions.
  • Keep the pile aerated and damp rather than soggy.
  • Avoid adding seriously diseased plants if your pile cannot reliably heat enough to kill pathogens.
Midwest tip: A larger or insulated pile can stay active longer, but a frozen compost pile is not ruined.

💧 5. Winterize Watering Equipment

Trapped water expands when frozen and can crack hoses, valves, filters and irrigation components.

  • Disconnect and completely drain garden hoses.
  • Empty watering cans, portable sprayers and rain barrels.
  • Remove timers and filters and store them somewhere protected.
  • Winterize irrigation systems according to manufacturer instructions.
  • Check exterior faucets and shutoff valves where applicable.
Midwest tip: Don't wait for weeks of freezing weather. A single hard freeze can damage equipment that contains water.

🛠️ 6. Clean & Store Garden Tools

  1. Brush or wash off soil.
  2. Dry all metal parts thoroughly.
  3. Remove surface rust where necessary.
  4. Sharpen blades according to tool instructions.
  5. Lubricate appropriate metal surfaces and moving joints.
  6. Inspect wooden handles for cracks or splinters.
  7. Store tools off damp floors in a protected space.

Check wheelbarrows, carts, trellises and plant supports while you're at it.

🥕 7. Check Stored Vegetables

Inspect your harvest regularly. Remove vegetables with mold, soft spots, off odors or freezing damage before deterioration spreads.

🌳 8. Protect Young Trees & Garden Structures

  • Install suitable trunk guards to protect young trees from rabbits and rodents.
  • Use fencing where deer browsing is a problem.
  • Water newly planted woody plants during dry spells while the ground remains unfrozen.
  • Check stakes, supports, trellises and fencing.
  • Secure lightweight structures against winter winds.
Midwest tip: Mulch the root zone, not the trunk. Never pile mulch into a “volcano” against bark.

🌿 9. Protect Your Garden Soil

Bare soil can be exposed to erosion, compaction and temperature swings. Protect it through winter.

  • Spread a loose layer of healthy shredded leaves over empty beds.
  • Add mature compost where soil improvement is needed.
  • Maintain appropriate fall-planted cover crops and plan their spring termination.
  • Inspect raised-bed frames and cover exposed soil.
  • Avoid unnecessary digging or tilling when soil is wet.
Midwest tip: Your garden doesn't need to be completely turned over in November. Protecting the soil surface is often the better choice.

📓 10. Review Your Garden & Plan Ahead

Before the details fade, write down your planting dates, best-producing varieties, pest problems, harvest successes and changes you'd make.

  • Which crops did best, and which struggled?
  • Did you start seeds or transplant too early or too late?
  • Which beds need more space or improved soil?
  • What tools, supplies or seeds need replacing?
  • What would you like to try next year?

Organize leftover seeds and start a wish list for next season.

❄️ November Tasks by USDA Zone

Hardiness zones describe average annual extreme minimum temperatures—not exact frost dates or soil-freeze dates. Adjust these suggestions to your forecast and local extension guidance.

Zone 3

Prioritize water-system winterizing, plant insulation, rodent protection and securing structures against snow and wind.

Zone 4

Check garlic and strawberry mulch timing, protect young berries and trees, and keep stored produce from freezing.

Zone 5

Watch freeze–thaw cycles, wait for strawberry dormancy, protect soil and water new woody plants when appropriate.

Zone 6

Use milder periods for cleanup and equipment maintenance. Mulch dormant plants as conditions warrant; plant spring bulbs where soil remains workable.

🌱 Looking Ahead: December

After the outdoor garden is tucked in, spend time reviewing seed catalogs, organizing seeds, checking seed-starting supplies, planning next year's beds, and growing herbs or microgreens indoors.

💚 A Final Thought

November gardening isn't about making every bed look perfect. It's about protecting what you've grown, caring for your soil and getting ready for another season. Some leaves and healthy stems can stay for wildlife and beneficial insects. That's part of gardening in the Midwest.

Happy gardening, and stay warm!
